## Metastable, precisely

A system is **metastable** when it sits in a *local* minimum of free energy
rather than the *global* one — stable against small disturbances, but not
truly at rest. Left alone for long enough, it still finds its way toward the
lower-energy state; metastability only says that path is slow.

An amorphous solid below its glass transition temperature is the textbook
case: molecularly disordered, kinetically frozen, and — on any timescale
short enough to matter to a person eating it — behaving as a rigid solid.
Given enough time (or enough moisture, or enough heat), it reorganises.
"Stale" is what that reorganisation looks like from the outside.

## The glass transition, restated

Module 9 introduced this for a youtiao crust forming during frying. The same
relationship runs the whole of today's lecture:

**T_g(w₂) = (w₁·T_g,dry + k·w₂·T_g,water) / (w₁ + k·w₂)**

- **w₁, w₂** — mass fractions of dry solid and water (w₁ + w₂ = 1)
- **T_g,dry** — glass transition temperature of the pure dry solid
- **T_g,water ≈ −135 °C** — glass transition of pure amorphous water, a
  standard value in the food glass-transition literature, not a fitted
  parameter
- **k** — Gordon-Taylor constant: how strongly the two components' free
  volumes mix, measured per material

## Same equation, opposite direction

Module 9 read this equation **forward in time during frying**: moisture
leaves the crust, so T_g *rises* past the crust's own temperature, and it
locks into glass.

Today reads it **backward, after cooking is over**: a crisp food sitting on
a bench has *already* crossed that boundary once. The only way its state
changes now is moisture coming back — and that has nothing to do with
frying, oven temperature, or any process variable from Stage 3. It has to do
with the air in the room.

## Water activity

A food's **water activity**, a_w, measures how readily it exchanges
moisture with its surroundings, on a scale where a_w = 1 is pure water and
a_w = 0 is bone dry. A food left in air at a given relative humidity
absorbs or loses moisture until its own a_w matches the air's:

**a_w,food ≈ RH_air / 100 at equilibrium**

This is the whole reason a sealed cracker stays crisp for weeks and an
opened one softens in a day: nothing about the cracker's temperature
changed. Its surroundings' relative humidity did — either because the
packaging that used to hold a_w fixed is gone, or because it was never
holding much back.

```notes
Emphasise: this is an equilibrium statement, not a rate. It tells you where
a specimen is headed, not how fast — that's the next slide.
```

## Moisture migration

Two distinct moisture-movement problems show up across this course, and
it's worth being precise about which one governs shelf life:

- **Internal migration** (Module 7's territory) — moisture moving *within*
  a food object, e.g. from a moist core outward to a drying crust during
  cooking. Driven by internal gradients, resolved on the timescale of
  cooking.
- **External exchange** (today's territory) — moisture moving *between* a
  food and the surrounding air, driven by the gap between the food's a_w
  and the air's RH. Resolved on the timescale of storage: hours to weeks,
  not minutes.

## Amorphous, not crystalline

Two food solids can share a chemical composition and behave completely
differently, because a glass transition is a property of **disordered**
matter. A crystal has a sharp melting point and no glass transition at all;
an amorphous solid — quenched or dried too fast for its molecules to
organise into a lattice — has neither a true melting point nor a fixed
"solid" state, only a T_g that moves with composition.

Most crisp food structures are amorphous by construction: a starch- or
gluten-based matrix, dried or fried quickly enough that it never
crystallises. That's precisely why the Gordon-Taylor picture applies to a
rice cracker or a spring roll wrapper, and not to, say, table salt.

---

## Why crisp fractures the way it does

Below T_g, a material is **glassy**: rigid, and — critically — brittle. Load
it, and it deforms almost imperceptibly right up until a crack nucleates and
runs through it in one uncontrolled event, releasing the elastic energy the
structure was storing. That single sharp failure, repeated at hundreds of
thin cell walls throughout a foam-like structure, is what a "crunch" *is* —
acoustically as well as mechanically.

Above T_g, the same matrix is **rubbery**: the same load instead produces
slow, ductile deformation with no sudden release. No crack, no sound, no
crunch — this is mechanically what "soft" and "stale" mean for a structure
whose composition hasn't changed at all.

## Necessary, not sufficient

Being glassy is necessary for "crisp" in the everyday sense, but not
sufficient: a thick glassy solid — hard candy — is rigid and brittle but
reads as *hard*, not crisp. Crispness also needs a thin, cellular or
foam-like structure that fractures audibly. The state diagram tells you
which side of T_g a material sits on; it says nothing about geometry, and
geometry is half of what "crisp" means to a person eating the food.

**Temperature (vertical) against moisture content (horizontal).**

## The instrument, live

The [Crispness Phase Diagram](/labs/crispness-phase-diagram/) puts exactly
this diagram in your hands:

1. Choose a specimen — rice cracker, spring roll wrapper, youtiao crust —
   each with its own T_g,dry, Gordon-Taylor k, and starting moisture.
2. Set an ambient relative humidity. This fixes the equilibrium moisture
   content the specimen is relaxing toward.
3. Advance time and watch the moisture-uptake curve climb, and the state
   point on the T_g diagram slide rightward.
4. Read the **stability margin** — the elapsed time at which the
   trajectory's T_g first drops to ambient temperature — the same crossing
   this lecture just described in words, computed exactly.

## Model limitations

Carried over from Modules 9 and 11, stated together because today is where
they compound:

- The crust/core boundary and the glass transition itself are treated as
  sharp; both occur over a range in a real food.
- The Gordon-Taylor equation is an **empirical mixing rule** — it fits real
  starch- and protein-water systems well, but k must be measured for a
  specific formulation, never assumed.
- The instrument's humidity-to-moisture relationship is a **linear sorption
  isotherm** — a clearly labelled illustrative stand-in for a measured GAB
  or BET isotherm, not a measurement of any specific food.

---

## Model limitations, continued

- Moisture uptake here is driven only by the gap between food and air; real
  uptake rate also depends on packaging resistance and internal structure,
  which this model doesn't include.
- None of this captures the geometric half of "crisp" — cell size, wall
  thickness, density — only the thermodynamic half.

## Where this points next

Module 12 asks the design question in reverse: given a target stability
margin, which of these free variables would you actually change, and by how
much?

- Raise T_g,dry — reformulate the dry solid itself (composition, degree of
  gelatinisation).
- Lower k — change which solid is doing the plasticising.
- Lower the starting moisture — change the drying or frying endpoint.
- Slow the approach to equilibrium — change the packaging, not the food.

The state diagram doesn't just describe a specimen's fate. It names exactly
which lever moves it.
