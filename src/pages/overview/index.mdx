## What this course is

SLOP3358 treats Chinese cooking as an applied soft-matter physics problem.
Across twelve weekly modules, grouped into four three-module stages, you
build the vocabulary and tools to describe a food's texture, structure and
behaviour under heat in the same terms a materials scientist would use for
any soft material: moduli, flow curves, transport coefficients, glass
transitions. The course reuses standard, established models — Newtonian and
power-law flow, Fourier conduction, Arrhenius kinetics, Flory–Stockmayer
percolation, the Gordon–Taylor equation — rather than inventing new theory.
Depth comes from applying real models honestly to real food systems, not
from piling on unfamiliar vocabulary.

The four stages run in a fixed order, each depending on the vocabulary the
last one built:

1. **Measuring Edible Matter** (Modules 1–3) — how to describe what a food
   material *is*: its mechanical state, its flow behaviour, its thermal
   signature.
2. **Structures and Networks** (Modules 4–6) — what gives a material its
   structure: gel networks, aggregated proteins, dispersed phases.
3. **Flow, Interfaces and Transformation** (Modules 7–9) — how that
   structure changes during cooking: heat and mass transport, reaction
   kinetics, phase transitions.
4. **Prediction and Material Design** (Modules 10–12) — fitting models to
   data, mapping a design space, and inverting the problem to engineer a
   target texture.

See the [module index](/sessions/) for the full week-by-week breakdown, and
[Assessment](/assessments/) for how the four pieces of graded work map onto
these stages.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of SLOP3358, you should be able to:

1. Identify which physical state (fluid, viscoelastic solid, amorphous
   glass, crystalline solid) a given cooked or processed food occupies, and
   justify that classification from its mechanical or thermal behaviour.
2. Select and apply an appropriate flow model (Newtonian, power-law,
   Herschel–Bulkley), thermal model (sensible or latent heat, Biot-number
   regime), or network model (percolation, Gordon–Taylor glass transition)
   to a specified food material, stating the governing equation and every
   variable in it.
3. Fit a model to a dataset, report a goodness-of-fit measure, and read that
   measure honestly — recognising when a model does not fit well rather
   than reporting a fit regardless.
4. State, for any quantitative claim you make about a food material,
   whether the underlying value is measured, drawn from published
   literature, or a labelled illustrative/synthetic estimate — and why that
   distinction matters.
5. Use a validated model in reverse: given a target texture or shelf-life
   outcome, propose a process or formulation change and justify it from the
   model rather than from taste alone.

## Prerequisites

A second-year course covering thermodynamics and either physical chemistry
or continuum mechanics (equivalent to a standard undergraduate
thermodynamics-and-kinetics sequence), plus enough calculus to be
comfortable with ordinary differential equations and basic curve fitting.
No prior cooking experience, food science background, or Chinese-language
knowledge is assumed — every food system used in the course is introduced
from first principles, in translation, before any model is applied to it.
